INTRODUCTION 
 
1.1 Project Background 
Modern external beam radiotherapy (EBRT) relies fundamentally on accurate patient anatom-
ical representations to deliver lethal ionizing radiation doses to target neoplastic lesions 
while strictly sparing adjacent critical organs-at-risk (OARs) [1, 2]. In contemporary clinical 
oncology, Computed Tomography (CT) serves as the indispensable cornerstone for treatment 
simulation, anatomical contouring, and radiation dose calculation [2]. The diagnostic values 
in CT images are natively calibrated in Hounsfield Units (HU), which maintain a direct, 
monotonic mathematical relationship with physical tissue electron densities (ρe) and photon 
linear attenuation coefficients (µ) [2, 3]: 
HU = 1000 µtissue − µwater (1.1) 
µwater 
 
Because high-energy photon and proton beams interact with human tissue predominantly 
through Compton scattering and secondary ionization processes, commercial Radiation 
Treatment Planning Systems (TPS) mandate CT-derived electron density calibration curves 
to execute Monte Carlo or collapsed-cone convolution dose calculations [2, 4]. 
Concurrently, Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) has emerged as an indispensable supple-
mentary diagnostic modality in neuro-oncology [3, 4]. By interrogating the nuclear magnetic 
resonance properties of hydrogen protons under static and radiofrequency magnetic fields, 
MRI generates exceptional soft-tissue contrast and superior spatial demarcation of intracra-
nial neoplasms, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) interfaces, and cranial nerve structures that remain 
indistinct or radiologically isodense on non-contrast CT [1, 4]. 
However, MRI signal intensity reflects nuclear spin-lattice ( T1) and spin-spin ( T2, T2∗) re- 
laxation dynamics, proton density, and local magnetic susceptibility gradients rather than 
physical electron density [2]. Consequently, standard MRI scans lack electron density infor-
mation and cannot be ingested directly by TPS algorithms for dose deposition modeling [1, 2].

1.2 Clinical Need for Synthetic CT (sCT) 
Because neither imaging modality alone satisfies both soft-tissue delineation and dose 
calculation prerequisites, current standard-of-care clinical radiotherapy enforces a dual-
modality workflow [2, 4]. In this paradigm, patients must undergo both an initial simulation 
CT scan (for electron density assignment) and a subsequent high-resolution diagnostic 
MRI scan (for target volume delineation). Medical physicists then execute multimodal 
image coregistration to warp the MR-delineated contours onto the reference planning CT 
geometry [2]. 
Despite its widespread clinical adoption, this dual-modality acquisition paradigm suffers 
from three critical vulnerabilities [2–4]: 
(1) Systematic Coregistration Errors: Inter-modality image registration between sepa-
rate CT and MRI scanners inevitably incurs spatial alignment uncertainties ranging 
from 1.5 mm to over 5.0 mm, even when rigid cranial immobilizers are employed [2, 3]. 
Anatomical variations—such as patient positioning discrepancies, physiological head 
flexion, soft-tissue deformation, and non-linear MRI magnetic gradient coil distor-
tions—introduce systematic positioning offsets [2]. In precision stereotactic radiosurgery 
(SRS) and intensity-modulated proton therapy (IMPT), where treatment margins often 
do not exceed 1 to 2 mm, registration misalignments can cause critical geometric misses 
of gross tumor volumes or unacceptable radiation overdose to the brainstem and optic 
chiasm [4]. 
(2) Cumulative Ionizing Radiation Exposure: Diagnostic and planning CT scans expose 
cancer patients to non-negligible ionizing radiation doses (typically 2 to 4 mSv per 
cranial CT examination) [2]. For pediatric neuro-oncology patients and individuals 
undergoing repeated adaptive radiotherapy fractions over multi-week protocols, this 
cumulative secondary radiation significantly elevates long-term risks of radiation-induced 
secondary malignancies [1, 2]. 
(3) Elevated Healthcare Costs and Clinical Overhead: The necessity of scheduling, acquir-
ing, reconstructing, and quality-assuring two independent scanning sessions substantially 
inflates healthcare expenditures, strains hospital infrastructure, and prolongs the crucial 
time interval between initial cancer diagnosis and radiation treatment delivery [2, 4].

To eliminate these clinical drawbacks, the international radiation oncology community is 
actively transitioning toward an “MRI-Only Radiotherapy Planning Workflow” [1, 2]. In 
an MRI-only pipeline, the planning CT scan is omitted entirely; instead, a highly accurate 
Synthetic CT (sCT)—also known as a pseudo-CT—is computationally synthesized directly 
from the planning MRI volume. This sCT preserves the pristine soft-tissue coordinate system 
of the MR scan while substituting geometrically aligned, quantitatively accurate Hounsfield 
Units for accurate dosimetric planning [1]. 
 
1.3 Technical Challenges in MRI-to-CT Translation 
Synthesizing realistic, radiologically valid CT volumes from raw MRI sequences presents 
severe computational and physical inverse-problem challenges [2, 5, 6]: 
• Severe Signal Ambiguity of Cortical Bone: The most prominent challenge in MRI-to-
CT translation is the acute signal ambiguity between cortical bone and air cavities [1, 2]. 
Cortical bone possesses an extremely short apparent transverse relaxation time (T2∗ < 
0.5 ms) and a low mobile proton density; as a result, free induction decay occurs faster than 
standard MRI echo times (TE), causing bone to manifest as void, zero-signal hypointense 
regions in standard T1-weighted gradient-echo or spin-echo sequences [2]. Similarly, air 
cavities (such as the sphenoid and ethmoid paranasal sinuses, mastoid air cells, and the 
ambient scanner bore) contain negligible proton density and appear completely dark [2, 3]. 
However, on CT, cortical bone and air represent the two opposing extremes of the attenua-
tion spectrum: air measures approximately −1000 HU, whereas dense skull bone exceeds 
+1000 to + 2500 HU [1]. Differentiating these identically dark MRI regions demands 
deep spatial contextual reasoning and structural geometric priors. 
• Ill-Posed, Non-Unique Cross-Modality Mapping: MRI voxel intensities are arbitrary 
scanner-dependent quantities governed by local coil sensitivities, radiofrequency trans-
mit/receive inhomogeneities (B+ and B− fields), receiver gain, and vendor-specific re- 1 1 
construction filters [1]. Consequently, MRI voxel values lack absolute physical units, 
rendering the translation f : MRI → CT an intrinsically non-injective, ill-posed inverse 
problem [5, 6]. 
• Geometric and Susceptibility Distortions: Static magnetic field ( B0) inhomogeneities 
at air-tissue interfaces cause localized voxel displacement and distortion artifacts in MRI,

1.5 Project Objectives 
The central goal of this project is to develop, benchmark, and clinically validate an end-
to-end deep learning framework for MRI-to-CT translation capable of enabling MRI-only 
radiotherapy planning. The specific measurable objectives are: 
1. Standardized Preprocessing and Cohort Stratification: Construct a robust, repro-
ducible data processing pipeline adhering to the SynthRAD2023 challenge protocol, 
incorporating robust intensity normalization ( [−1000 HU, +3000 HU] → [−1.0, +1.0]), 
cranial mask isolation, and rigorous patient-level 80%/20% split isolation (Seed 42) to 
prevent data leakage. 
2. Rigorous 5-Model Comparative Implementation: Implement, train to convergence, 
and evaluate five diverse architectural families spanning deep convolutional regression 
(ResUNet), generative adversarial networks (Pix2Pix cGAN), unconstrained conditional 
diffusion (cDDPM), attention-guided diffusion (SOTA-CDDPM), and the proposed two-
stage Cross-Conditioned Residual Diffusion (CCR-Diffusion). 
3.  Multi-Faceted Clinical Metric Benchmarking: Evaluate synthesized CT slices across 
four primary quantitative metrics: Mean Absolute Error across all intracranial tissues 
(MAEall), Bone-specific reconstruction error (MAEbone, HU > 300), Structural Similarity 
Index Measure (SSIM), and Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR), aggregated across both 
slice-level and patient-level distributions. 
4. Computational and Hardware Profiling: Measure real-world clinical feasibility by 
profiling each model’s total parameter footprint, trainable parameters, single-slice infer-
ence latency (milliseconds per slice), frames per second (FPS), and peak GPU VRAM 
utilization under deterministic DDIM sampling (50 steps). 
5. Ablation and Radiotherapy Dosimetric Validation: Validate the superiority of two-
stage residual diffusion over standard GAN and DDPM models, demonstrating significant 
reductions in cortical bone error (MAE bone) and eliminating hallucinated anatomical 
artifacts.

LITERATURE SURVEY 
 
2.1 Introduction to the Domain Literature 
The quest to achieve an accurate, robust, and clinically deployable MRI-only radiotherapy 
workflow has inspired intensive multidisciplinary research spanning medical physics, clinical 
oncology, computer vision, and deep generative learning over the past two decades [2]. The 
central objective of MRI-to-CT translation is to infer quantitatively reliable Hounsfield Units 
(HU) and spatial electron density distributions ( ρe) directly from non-ionizing magnetic 
resonance acquisitions, thereby resolving the dual-modality registration dilemmas inherent 
to modern treatment planning [1, 2]. 
Methodologies developed in the literature can be broadly categorized into five successive 
evolutionary generations: 
(1) Classical Heuristic Methods (Pre-Deep Learning): Including bulk-density assignment 
through multi-tissue thresholding, fuzzy classification, and single/multi-atlas deformable 
image registration [15–20]. 
(2) Deep Convolutional Regression Architectures: Employing supervised voxel-wise loss 
formulations ( L1, L2, Huber) over fully convolutional architectures, such as standard 
U-Nets, 3D CNNs, and Residual U-Nets [7, 8, 21–26]. 
(3) Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs): Leveraging minimax game dynamics with 
PatchGAN discriminators to synthesize high-frequency structural textures in paired 
(Pix2Pix) and unpaired (CycleGAN, MedGAN) imaging regimes [9, 10, 27–32]. 
(4) Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs): Establishing non-adversarial, 
likelihood-based generative formulations capable of modeling complex anatomical prob-
ability distributions via iterative stochastic denoising and deterministic DDIM trajecto-
ries [6, 11–13, 33–36]. 
(5) Hybrid, Residual, and Feature-Modulated Generative Models: Decoupling global 
structural prediction from high-frequency residual diffusion using Feature-wise Linear 
Modulation (FiLM) and latent representations [1, 5, 14, 37, 38].

2.2 Classical Atlas-Based and Bulk-Density Assignment Ap-
proaches 
2.2.1 Bulk Tissue Density Assignment (HU Override) 
 
Early investigations in MRI-based radiotherapy relied on direct tissue segmentation combined 
with bulk Hounsfield Unit overriding [2, 15]. In this heuristic framework, the patient’s 
planning MRI is segmented into a small number of discrete tissue classes (typically 3 
to 5 compartments: air, lung, soft tissue, spongy bone, and dense cortical bone) using 
intensity thresholding, fuzzy c-means clustering, or dual-echo ultrashort echo time (UTE) 
sequences [15, 18]. Each segmented voxel is subsequently assigned a uniform, literature-
standard CT number (for example, −1000 HU for air, +40 HU for cranial soft tissue, and 
+1000 HU for cortical bone). 
While computationally trivial and conceptually straightforward, bulk-density assignment 
models suffer from fundamental physical approximations [2]: 
• Neglect of Intra-Tissue Density Heterogeneity: Real human bone possesses complex 
trabecular architecture, diploic spaces, and varying mineralization gradients. Enforcing a 
homogeneous HU override obliterates intra-organ density variations, leading to systematic 
dose calculation discrepancies of 2% to 5% in photon therapy and exceeding 10% in proton 
Bragg peak range estimations [2, 4]. 
• Segmentation Boundary Sensitivity: Any misclassification at tissue interfaces (such 
as classifying thin skull bone as air due to partial volume effects) introduces severe step-
function density errors that propagate directly into radiation dose calculations [3].

introduced [16,17,19,20]. In single-atlas approaches, a pre-acquired exemplar patient dataset 
containing perfectly co-registered MRI and CT volumes is used as a template. Non-rigid

2.2.3 Fundamental Limitations of Classical Approaches 
 
Despite delivering continuous attenuation distributions superior to bulk overrides, atlas-based 
methods exhibit critical clinical shortcomings [1, 2]: 
(1) Inability to Model Atypical and Pathological Anatomies: Deformable registration 
assumes topological homeomorphism between atlas and patient. In patients presenting 
with massive intracranial tumors, midline shifts, craniotomy defects, or post-surgical 
resection cavities, non-rigid registration inevitably fails, producing distorted anatomical 
hallucinations [2]. 
(2) Extreme Computational Latency: Multi-atlas non-rigid registrations across large 
3D patient volumes require substantial iterative optimization, routinely consuming 30 
minutes to 2 hours per patient volume, which is incompatible with online adaptive 
radiotherapy workflows [16].

2.3 Deep Convolutional Regression Networks 
 
2.3.1 Fully Convolutional Networks and U-Net Architectures 
 
The advent of deep convolutional neural networks revolutionized medical image-to-image 
translation [8, 21, 22]. Han (2017) demonstrated the first end-to-end deep learning framework 
for cranial synthetic CT generation using a deep convolutional autoencoder architecture [21]. 
By learning hierarchical spatial feature representations directly from paired MRI and CT 
voxels, the network mapped complex non-linear intensity relationships without explicit tissue 
segmentation. 
The quintessential architecture for biomedical translation is the U-Net [8], comprising an 
encoder contracting path that captures contextual semantic abstraction, a symmetric decoder 
expanding path that enables precise spatial localization, and direct skip connections between 
opposing hierarchical levels. Skip connections concatenate high-resolution feature maps 
from encoder stages directly to decoder upsampling stages, preserving fine spatial edge 
boundaries that would otherwise be lost through pooling or strided convolutions [8, 24].

due to vanishing and exploding gradient phenomena [39]. To stabilize optimization and

formulated the Deep Residual U-Net (ResUNet). Within each convolutional block, identity 
shortcut connections are introduced:

2.3.3 The Regression-to-the-Mean Phenomenon and High-Frequency 
Loss 
Despite yielding high Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR) values and low average Mean 
Absolute Error (MAE), direct convolutional regression networks suffer from a well-known 
mathematical artifact termed regression-to-the-mean [9, 12]. When optimized using pixel-
wise L1 or L2 objectives: 
θ∗ = arg min Ex,y [  y − fθ(x)  p] , p ∈ {1, 2} (2.5) 
 
the network estimates the conditional expectation E[y|x] (for p = 2) or conditional median 
(for p = 1) across all plausible target textures consistent with input x [9]. In areas of high 
structural ambiguity—such as complex cranial sutures, mastoid air cell honeycombs, and 
inner ear labyrinths—the model averages multiple conflicting high-frequency configurations. 
This mathematical averaging manifests as severe structural oversmoothing and attenuation 
underestimation in cortical bone, which directly perturbs dose calculations at bone-soft tissue 
interfaces [2].

A pivotal innovation of Pix2Pix is the Markovian PatchGAN discriminator [9]. Rather than 
evaluating the entire image as a single global real/fake scalar, the PatchGAN restricts its 
receptive field to local N × N image patches (e.g., 70 × 70 voxels), classifying whether 
each sub-patch represents a realistic cross-modality pairing. By penalizing local structural 
incongruities, the PatchGAN functions as a learned high-frequency texture loss, compelling 
Gθ to recover realistic bone edges and cranial boundaries [10, 29, 31]. 
 
2.4.2 Unpaired Cross-Modality Translation (CycleGAN) 
 
While Pix2Pix mandates strictly registered paired training slices, acquiring geometrically 
congruent MRI-CT datasets is challenging in pelvic and thoracic sites due to physiological 
peristalsis, rectal filling, and respiratory deformation [27]. To circumvent rigid registration

(CycleGAN). Operating on unpaired datasets X ⊂ MRI and Y ⊂ CT, CycleGAN couples

2.4.3 Pathological Hallucinations and Dosimetric Vulnerabilities of GANs 
 
Although GAN-based sCT models produce visually compelling images with sharp edges, 
their deployment in clinical radiation oncology remains intensely scrutinized due to grave 
safety concerns [1, 2, 5]: 
• Adversarial Hallucination Artifacts: The adversarial objective optimizes perceptual 
plausibility rather than physical photon attenuation truth [5]. Consequently, GAN genera-
tors are prone to hallucinating fictitious bone calcifications, missing pathological tumor 
lesions, or fabricating non-existent air pockets to satisfy the discriminator’s local texture 
criteria [1, 32]. 
• Mode Collapse and Training Instability: Adversarial optimization represents an unstable 
saddle-point search prone to limit cycles, mode collapse, and hyperparameter sensitiv-

2.5 Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models in Medical Imag-

2.5.1 Foundations of Forward and Reverse Gaussian Processes 
 
Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs) have recently emerged as the preemi-
nent generative modeling paradigm, systematically outperforming GANs in image quality,

2.6 Feature Conditioning and Residual Diffusion Mechanisms 
 
2.6.1 Decoupled Coarse-to-Fine Architectures 
 
To alleviate the representational burden on diffusion models, recent investigations explore 
decoupled coarse-to-fine paradigms [5, 14, 38]. In naive conditional DDPM, the network 
must simultaneously perform two disparate tasks: 
1. Learning global anatomical layout and soft-tissue organ boundaries from scratch. 
2. Synthesizing fine-grained Hounsfield Unit variations and cortical bone microstructures. 
 
By decoupling these responsibilities into a deterministic Stage-1 predictor and a generative 
Stage-2 residual refiner, the diffusion model is relieved of reconstructing macro-scale anatomy, 
concentrating its modeling capacity solely on localized high-frequency residuals [14]. 
 
2.6.2 Feature-Wise Linear Modulation (FiLM) 
Integrating conditioning information solely via early channel concatenation (as in Palette) 
often leads to conditional neglect, where the deep layers of the denoiser lose alignment 
with the conditioning MRI features [14]. Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM) [14] 
overcomes this by applying affine transformations to intermediate activation maps throughout 
the network: 
FiLM(F; γ, β) = (1.0 + γ(c)) ⊙ F + β(c) (2.15) 
 
where γ and β are adaptive scaling and shifting vectors dynamically predicted from the 
conditioning structural features c via 1 × 1 convolutional projections. FiLM ensures that 
anatomical geometry actively gates and modulates feature representations at every 
hierarchical depth of the generative denoiser. Unlike naive input concatenation—which 
treats spatial anatomical priors as passive early-stage channels—multi-scale FiLM layers 
inject hierarchical conditioning directly into the bottleneck and decoding blocks at 
resolutions of 16×16, 32×32, and 64×64. By adaptively scaling and shifting normalized 
feature activations, the network dynamically amplifies high-frequency calvarial bone 
textures while suppressing stochastic variance across homogeneous brain parenchyma. 
Consequently, the diffusion trajectory is strictly bounded by the macroscopic coordinates

2.8 Critical Research Gaps and Problem Formulation Insights 
A rigorous synthesis of the 35 surveyed studies reveals four fundamental research gaps that 
directly motivate the experimental benchmark and architectural designs of this major project: 
Gap 1: Absence of Standardized, Multi-Model Benchmarking Under Identical Con-
ditions: Although literature reports diverse models spanning CNNs, GANs, and 
DDPMs, direct performance comparisons across papers are compromised by di-
vergent datasets, proprietary normalization windows, inconsistent split isolation 
strategies, and differing anatomical evaluation regions. There exists an acute need 
for a rigorous, unified benchmark evaluating ResUNet, Pix2Pix, cDDPM, Attention-
CDDPM, and Residual Diffusion under strictly identical patient splits (Seed 42) and 
evaluation metrics (MAEall, MAEbone, SSIM, PSNR, FPS, VRAM). 
Gap 2: The Core Dilemma Between Regression Blur and Adversarial Hallucination: 

=== PAGE 24 ===
Deep Learning-Based MRI-TO-CT Image Translation Using Generative Models 
Dept of CSE, JIT, Bengaluru  2026–2027 Page 24 
 
 
Pure regression architectures (ResUNet) guarantee global stability and zero halluci-
nations but fail to accurately synthesize dense cortical bone structures (+1000 to + 
2500 HU) due to regression-to-the-mean. Conversely, conditional GANs (Pix2Pix) 
synthesize sharp bone textures but remain vulnerable to unpredictable structural 
hallucinations, presenting severe safety hazards in radiation treatment planning. 
Gap 3: Unconstrained Diffusion Drift and Sampling Latency in Clinical Workflows: 
While conditional DDPMs resolve GAN instability and generate high-fidelity distri-
butions, unconstrained diffusion over full dynamic ranges ([−1000 HU, +3000 HU]) 
suffers from stochastic drift in background and soft tissues. Moreover, standard 
DDPM sampling latency remains clinically prohibitive. 
Gap 4: Decoupling Macro-Anatomy from High-Frequency Residual Diffusion: Exist-
ing medical diffusion models force a single network to concurrently resolve both 
macro-scale organ geometries and fine microstructural bone calcifications. A de-
coupled architecture—wherein a deterministic Stage-1 network establishes a coarse 
anatomical prior while a FiLM-guided Stage-2 diffusion model denoises normalized 
high-frequency residuals—represents an unexplored, theoretically sound solution to 
achieve both structural invariance and bone fidelity. 
These identified gaps establish the technical foundation for the system architecture, mathe-
matical foundations, and implementation details presented in Chapter 3.

3.3 Dataset Architecture and Preprocessing Pipeline 
 
3.3.1 SynthRAD2023 Grand Challenge Brain Dataset 
 
The models are evaluated on the public SynthRAD2023 Grand Challenge cranial dataset [1]. 
The cohort comprises high-resolution paired T1-weighted Magnetic Resonance Imaging 
(MRI) and corresponding Computed Tomography (CT) scans acquired from multi-institutional 
clinical radiotherapy centers. All volumetric acquisitions undergo rigid skull-base coregis-
tration and reslicing to an isotropic spatial resolution of 1.0 × 1.0 × 1.0 mm3 with standard 
256 × 256 in-plane slice matrix dimensions. 
 
3.3.2 Data Leakage Prevention and Cohort Splitting 
 
To guarantee rigorous statistical independence and prevent inter-slice data leakage between 
training and testing distributions: 
• All data splits are performed strictly at the **patient cohort level** rather than random 
slice-wise mixing. 
• An 80%/20% stratified patient-level partitioning is executed under a deterministic random 
seed (Seed = 42). 
• The held-out test cohort comprises exactly 363 axial paired 2D slices spanning all cranial 
levels, from the posterior cranial fossa and skull base through the ventricular system to the 
superior vertex.

4.4 Training Hyperparameters and Optimization Protocol 
All five models were trained using PyTorch 2.2.0 with CUDA 12.1 acceleration on dedicated 
NVIDIA GPUs. Table 4.1 provides the comprehensive hyperparameter configurations across 
all architectures.To ensure fair empirical comparisons and reproducible training 
dynamics, uniform input dimensions of 256 × 256 pixels and a mini-batch size of 8 slices 
were maintained across all five experimental pipelines. The AdamW optimizer was 
employed with a weight decay coefficient of 1.0 × 10 ⁻⁴ to reduce overfitting across deep 
bottleneck convolutional layers. A cosine annealing learning rate schedule was used for 
all architectures, reducing the initial learning rate from 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ for feedforward 
regressors and 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ for diffusion backbones to a minimum learning rate of 1.0 × 
10⁻⁶. This scheduling strategy facilitates smooth convergence without abrupt learning rate 
changes. Numerical stability was further enhanced using Automatic Mixed Precision 
(AMP) with FP16 and global gradient clipping with a maximum gradient norm of 1.0 to 
prevent gradient explosions during reverse diffusion updates. The diffusion models were 
trained using 1,000 discrete noise steps with a linear variance schedule, starting at β₁ = 
0.0001 and ending at β₁₀₀₀ = 0.02. During inference, a deterministic 50-step DDIM 
sampler with η = 0 was employed to support efficient MRI-to-synthetic CT translation. 
The proposed CCR-Diffusion model followed a two-stage training strategy, consisting of 
40 epochs of Stage 1 coarse anatomical pre-training, followed by 80 epochs of Stage 2 
residual FiLM denoiser optimization. The final model checkpoints were selected based on 
the minimum validation loss.

5.7 Discussion: Overcoming Regression Blur and GAN Halluci-
nation 
5.7.1 Why Pure Regression Models Blur: The Conditional Mean Trap 
Standard supervised convolutional networks trained with L1 or L2 pixel loss (such as Model 1: 
ResUNet) inherently converge toward the conditional expectation of the target distribution [7, 
10]: 
f ∗(x) = arg min Ex,y [  y − f (x)  2] = E[y | x] (5.1) 
 
Because MRI-to-CT translation is ill-posed—meaning a single MRI voxel intensity can corre-
spond to multiple physical CT attenuation values depending on sub-voxel mineralization—the 
network averages all plausible target values. In high-frequency regions (e.g., bone-soft tissue 
boundaries and skull sutures), this mathematical averaging produces oversmoothed, low-pass 
filtered reconstructions that lack sharp gradient transitions.

5.7.2 Why Conditional GANs Pose Clinical Hallucination Risks 
 
Conditional GANs (Model 2: Pix2Pix) address blurriness by introducing an adversarial 
discriminator D, which forces the generator to sample from the edge of the data manifold 
rather than predicting the blurry conditional mean [9]. However, adversarial training lacks 
strict monotonicity constraints. In clinical radiotherapy, this introduces two dangerous 
vulnerabilities: 
1. Pseudo-Calcification Hallucinations: The generator may synthesize fictitious high-
density bone flecks or calcifications within brain tissue simply to satisfy the discriminator’s 
high-frequency texture requirements. If present in a radiotherapy plan, an artificial 
calcification would cause the TPS algorithm to erroneously overestimate radiation beam 
attenuation, leading to dangerous target tumor underdosing. 
2. Mode Dropping and Distortion: The discriminator operates on local 70 × 70 patches, 
rendering it blind to global geometric relationships and causing subtle non-rigid distortions 
along the skull base. 
 
5.7.3 How CCR-Diffusion Eliminates Diffusion Drift and Preserves Ge-
ometry 
Standard conditional DDPMs (Model 3) generate synthetic images starting from uncon-
strained white Gaussian noise yT ∼ N(0, I). In medical translation, forcing a denoiser to 
solve both low-frequency anatomical geometry (where the head is located) and high-frequency 
CT numbers (bone density) simultaneously from random noise incurs high variance and 
requires large numbers of reverse steps. 
The proposed CCR-Diffusion Network (Model 5) resolves this fundamental dilemma 
through three decoupled mechanisms: 
1. Stage 1 Anatomical Anchoring: A deterministic supervised ResUNet establishes macro-
scopic cranial anatomy, soft-tissue boundaries, and global coordinate alignment, eliminat-
ing any risk of geometric hallucination. 
2. FiLM Multi-Scale Structural Modulation: Feature-wise Linear Modulation dynam-
ically scales ( γ) and shifts ( β) intermediate diffusion feature maps using hierarchical 
features extracted from the anchor network. This informs the denoiser of spatial anatomical

3. Normalized High-Frequency Residual Diffusion: The diffusion model is tasked solely

stochastic reverse sampling to high-frequency textural corrections and bone mineralization 
details, CCR-Diffusion achieves rapid convergence, eliminates diffusion drift, preserves 
global geometric stability, and delivers unprecedented clinical accuracy (0.8864 Masked

CONCLUSION AND FUTURE SCOPE 
 
6.1 Summary of Project Work 
This project addressed the critical clinical and computational challenges of transitioning 
modern radiation oncology toward an MRI-Only Radiotherapy Planning Workflow. By 
synthesizing accurate, geometrically faithful synthetic Computed Tomography (sCT) volumes 
directly from magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), the need for planning CT simulation 
scans can be eliminated—eradicating inter-modality coregistration misalignments (1–5 mm), 
preventing unnecessary ionizing radiation burdens in cancer patients, and significantly 
streamlining clinical workflows. 
Formulated on the international SynthRAD2023 Grand Challenge cranial cohort (comprising 
60 clinical brain patients and 3,614 paired 2D axial slices), this work implemented, trained, 
and benchmarked five distinct generative deep learning architectures under strictly identical 
patient-level splits (Seed = 42, 80%/20%) and standardized evaluation protocols: 
1. Model 1: Deep Residual U-Net (ResUNet Baseline)—Supervised convolutional regres-
sion baseline optimized via composite masked L1 and Mean Squared Error losses. 
2. Model 2: Conditional GAN (Pix2Pix)—Paired adversarial translation framework featur- 
ing a U-Net Generator and a 70 × 70 PatchGAN Discriminator. 
3. Model 3: Conditional DDPM From Scratch—Likelihood-based generative diffusion 
model trained directly on paired MRI-CT distributions. 
4. Model 4: SOTA-CDDPM with Residual Attention —Conditional diffusion model 
enhanced with cross-layer self-attention gating and an anatomical bone-weighted hybrid 
objective (wbone = 5.0). 
5. Model 5: Proposed Cross-Conditioned Residual Diffusion (CCR-Diffusion)—A novel 
two-stage decoupled architecture featuring a deterministic Stage 1 ResUNet coarse anatom-
ical anchor coupled with a Stage 2 diffusion denoiser operating on standardized residuals 
dynamically modulated via Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM).

6.2 Key Findings and Benchmark Insights 
Comprehensive quantitative benchmarking and hardware profiling across the 363 held-out 
test slices established several decisive scientific and clinical findings: 
• CCR -Diffusion Achieves Landmark Performance: The proposed CCR-Diffusion archi-
tecture emerged as the definitive winner across all four quantitative clinical metrics, attain-
ing an overall MAEall of 48.2 ± 14.6 HU, a bone-specific MAEbone of 98.4 ± 19.2 HU, 
a Structural Similarity Index (SSIM) of 0.9248 ± 0.0215, a Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio 
(PSNR) of 26.85 ± 1.94 dB, and a Bone Dice coefficient of 0.8245 ± 0.0482. 
• Resolution of the Regression-Blur Dilemma: Standard supervised regressors (Model 1) 
converge to the conditional expectation E[y | x], leading to oversmoothed bone boundaries 
and elevated bone errors (168.4 HU). CCR-Diffusion overcomes this fundamental limit by 
tasking its diffusion denoiser exclusively with residual high-frequency fine-tuning. 
• Elimination of Adversarial Hallucination Risks: While conditional GANs (Model 2) 
produce visually sharp images, they are prone to mode collapse and fictitious pseudo-
calcifications that pose grave risks of beam underdosing in radiotherapy. By strictly 
anchoring macroscopic geometry with the Stage 1 network, CCR-Diffusion guarantees 
absolute anatomical boundary consistency. 
• Resolution of Air vs. Bone Hypointensity Ambiguity: In raw T1-weighted MRI, cortical 
bone (T2∗ < 0.5 ms) and air cavities both present as dark, signal-void regions. While 
baseline models frequently misclassified paranasal sinuses as intermediate bone, CCR-

leveraging multi-scale structural priors from the anchor encoder. 
• Clinical Real-Time Feasibility: Utilizing a 50-step deterministic Denoising Diffusion 
Implicit Model (DDIM, η = 0) reverse sampling schedule, CCR-Diffusion achieves an 
inference throughput of approximately 2.01 FPS (498 ms per slice). A full 3D cranial radio-

6.3 Limitations of the Current Work 
Despite its significant advancements, the current study possesses several limitations that 
warrant consideration: 
1. Two-Dimensional In-Plane Slice Processing: The current framework operates on inde-
pendent 2D axial slices. Although in-plane anatomical structures are reconstructed with 
high fidelity, slice-by-slice synthesis can introduce subtle inter-slice step discontinuities 
along the coronal and sagittal reformations. 
2. Computational Latency vs. Pure Feedforward Models: Although 50-step DDIM 
reduces sampling time by 20× compared to 1,000-step DDPM, an inference latency 
of 498 ms per slice is still substantially slower than single-pass feedforward networks 
(18.4 ms). This latency may limit application in real-time inter-fraction beam gating 
during MRI-Linac treatment delivery. 
3. Provisional HU Calibration Window: Experiments were conducted using normalized 
dynamic ranges mapped to the SynthRAD2023 cranial window [−1000 HU, +1500 HU]. 
True clinical commissioning requires cross-validation against physical electron density 
tissue phantoms (CIRS phantoms) acquired across diverse clinical scanner vendors. 
 
6.4 Future Research Scope 
The promising results achieved by CCR-Diffusion open several compelling avenues for future 
technical innovation and clinical translation: 
1. Three-Dimensional Volumetric and Latent Diffusion Models (LDMs): Extending 
CCR-Diffusion to 3D convolutional or Latent Diffusion topologies to operate natively on 
3D spatial volumes, guaranteeing seamless volumetric continuity across all orthogonal 
planes (axial, sagittal, coronal). 
2. Few-Step Consistency Models and Rectified Flow: Investigating Consistency Distilla-
tion (CD) and Rectified Flow algorithms to compress the diffusion reverse trajectory from 
50 steps down to 1–4 steps, enabling ultra-fast real-time inference (< 20 ms per slice) for 
online adaptive MRI-guided radiotherapy on MRI-Linac systems. 
3. End-to-End Dosimetric Validation and Gamma Analysis: Ingesting the generated 
sCT volumes directly into commercial treatment planning systems (e.g., Eclipse, RaySta-

tion, Monaco) to compute full 3D Monte Carlo radiation dose distributions. Evaluating 
clinical gamma passing rates (γ3%/3mm > 98% and γ2%/2mm > 95%) and Dose-Volume 
Histograms (DVHs) on Planning Target Volumes (PTVs) and critical Organs-at-Risk 
(brainstem, optic nerves, and chiasm). 
4. Multi-Contrast MRI Ingestion: Extending the conditioning framework to ingest com-
plementary MRI sequences ( T1-weighted, T2-weighted, and FLAIR) to provide rich, 
orthogonal tissue contrast for enhanced anatomical delineation. 
5. Cross -Anatomical and Multi-Center Generalization: Validating the CCR-Diffusion 
framework on other challenging anatomical sites—including pelvic prostate cohorts 
(SynthRAD2023 Task 2) and head-and-neck cancer patients—to establish a unified, 
generalizable clinical synthetic CT engine. 
 
6.5 Concluding Remarks 
In conclusion, this project successfully formulated, implemented, and benchmarked five 
deep learning paradigms for MRI-to-CT translation, demonstrating the decisive superiority 
of the proposed CCR-Diffusion framework. By marrying deterministic structural anchoring 
with high-frequency residual diffusion, CCR-Diffusion resolves the long-standing dichotomy 
between regression blur and adversarial hallucinations. This research establishes a solid, 
reproducible, and clinically viable foundation for single-modality MRI-only radiotherapy, 
bringing safer, more accurate, and radiation-free cancer treatment planning closer to routine 
clinical practice.
